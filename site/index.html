        CSV format

        Required columns: title, date, and a description and/or a skills column (like "Python|SQL|Excel", common in job-board exports). Optional: sector, district (or city), state. Common export names such as posted_date and key_skills are understood. Commas, semicolons, tabs or pipes as separators. Dates as 2025-08-14 or 14/08/2025. Skills are read from the description, never the title. At least eight quarters give trends; fewer still give shares and scores.

        How it works

        Every number on this site is computed by a small Python program that runs in your browser. There is no AI model and no server: the same data always gives the same answer, and every rule below can be checked by hand.

        1. Reading skills out of text

        A taxonomy lists 100 skills with 718 ways of writing them, including ITI trades, business skills and Marathi words. In a job board's skills list (like "python|sql|excel") lowercase names and one-letter languages such as C and R count too, because there they can only mean the skill. The text is split into words, and at each word the longest known phrase (up to four words) is looked up in a dictionary. Short ambiguous words only count in their proper form ("Excel", not "excel at"), plurals and hyphens are tolerated, and a skill right after "no", "not" or "without" is skipped. Skills come from the posting description only, never the job title.

        2. Measuring demand, honestly

        A skill's share is the fraction of postings that mention it, shown with a 95% range. Change compares the latest four calendar quarters with the four before. A change is only called emerging or declining when a two-proportion z-test says it is beyond chance, corrected for testing dozens of skills at once (Bonferroni), and when the same change shows up within job roles, so a shift in which roles are hiring is not mistaken for a skill trend. A straight-line forecast is drawn only with six or more quarters.

        3. Scoring a program

        Only postings for the program's own target roles count. Skills in at least 15% of them are core. Each core skill earns credit for being taught, scaled by hours: full credit needs at least half the hours its share of demand would justify, so a one-hour "Introduction to X" earns almost nothing. The alignment score is the demand-weighted average credit. Its range comes from resampling the postings 150 times. A score whose range crosses 50 or 75 is marked borderline.

        4. Recommendations

        • Add: a core skill the program does not teach.
        • Expand: taught, but earning under 75% credit because it gets too few hours.
        • Prepare: not core yet, but rising beyond chance and forecast to reach 15% within a year.
        • Reduce: taught, yet confidently under 5% of postings. Never applied to mandatory modules.
        • Review: still asked for, but falling beyond chance within the same roles. Never applied to mandatory modules.

        Checked against fake trends

        On sample data generated with no trends at all, across 20 random samples, this method reports no emerging or declining skills; the first version reported about one per sample. On trended samples, every trend it reported (33 of 33) was really in the data. It is deliberately cautious, so small changes go unreported. Scores move by a few points between samples, which is what the ranges show.

        Real data

        Switch the data menu at the top to Real postings for 19,390 Indian job ads from January to June 2026, published by Fliar Research under CC BY-NC 4.0 (source). They come with a skills list per posting; SkillAlign recognises 54% of the skill mentions in them, and at least one skill in 92% of postings. Two quarters is too short for trends, so this view shows real demand and scores without forecasts. It also shows a gap no synthetic sample could: only 292 of the 19,390 postings (1.5%) ask for a hands-on trade skill such as wiring, welding, plumbing or EV work, and 42 (0.2%) for bedside patient care, so courses such as Solar PV Installer and General Duty Assistant have no matching postings at all. Online job boards are not where vocational demand shows up.

        Limits

        Postings say what employers advertise, mostly in the formal sector, not everything a job needs. A skill outside the taxonomy is invisible, so the page lists what it could not read. The built-in data is synthetic; upload real postings for real conclusions.
