        CSV format

        Required columns: title, date, and a description and/or a skills column (like "Python|SQL|Excel", common in job-board exports). Optional: sector, district (or city), state. Common export names such as posted_date and key_skills are understood. With an employer column, identical reposts are counted once and score ranges account for each company's postings moving together. Commas, semicolons, tabs or pipes as separators. Dates as 2025-08-14 or 14/08/2025. Skills are read from the description, never the title. At least eight quarters give trends; fewer still give shares and scores.

        How it works

        Every number on this site is computed by a small Python program that runs in your browser. There is no AI model and no server: the same data always gives the same answer, and every rule below can be checked by hand.

        1. Reading skills out of text

        A taxonomy lists 101 skills with 820 ways of writing them, including ITI trades, business skills and Marathi words. In running text the longest known phrase of up to four words is looked up at each word; short ambiguous words count only in their proper form ("Excel", not "excel at"), and a skill right after "no", "not" or "without" is skipped. A job board's skills list ("python|sql|excel") is read item by item, and one-letter names like C or R count only when they are a whole item. Skills never come from the job title. Words it cannot read are listed for you.

        2. Measuring demand, honestly

        A skill's share is the fraction of postings that mention it, shown with a 95% range. Change compares the latest four calendar quarters with the four before, using a test stratified by job role (Mantel-Haenszel): the same role is compared with itself, so a change in which roles are hiring is never read as a skill trend. The threshold is corrected for testing many skills at once (Bonferroni, over skills in at least 5% of postings), and if most recent postings are in roles absent a year earlier, no trend is called. A forecast with a 95% range is drawn only with six or more quarters.

        3. Scoring a program

        Only postings for the program's own target roles count, and an entry-level course is compared with entry-level jobs only. With fewer than 30 matching postings the program is not scored and gets no advice. Skills in at least 15% of the postings are core. Each core skill earns credit for being taught, scaled by hours: full credit needs half of what a 200-hour reference course would give it in proportion to its demand, and never less than 5 hours. So shrinking a course lowers its score, padding it with unrelated hours changes nothing, and adding hours to a core skill can only help. The alignment score is the demand-weighted average credit. Its 90% range comes from resampling employers 400 times, re-picking the core skills each time, because one company's postings move together.

        4. Recommendations

        • Add: a core skill the program does not teach.
        • Expand: taught, but with fewer hours than full credit needs.
        • Prepare: not core yet, but rising beyond chance and forecast to reach 15% within a year.
        • Reduce: taught, yet under 5% of postings and confidently under 15%.
        • Review: still asked for, but falling beyond chance within the same roles.

        Nothing taught in a compulsory module, and no basic skill (communication, English, teamwork, problem solving, computer use), is ever advised to cut: job ads under-report basics.

        Checked against the truth

        On synthetic data the true trends are known, so the method can be scored (full results). On trend-free data it reports almost no trends, even when the mix of job roles shifts or employers repost ads, where the first version reported nearly five per sample. It finds every large change and few modest ones; what it reports points the right way 98% of the time. The 90% score ranges contained the true score 85 to 100% of the time, and forecasts were within their 95% range 91% of the time in a backtest.

        Real data

        Switch the data menu to Real postings for 19,194 Indian job ads from January to June 2026, published by Fliar Research under CC BY-NC 4.0 (source), cleaned as described in the project's data notes. The source is a 6% sample that keeps every posting of a hash-chosen set of companies, collected unevenly over time, so its numbers describe that sample, not the whole job market. Two quarters is too short for trends. The skill list was grown from these postings, so it recognises 50% of their skill items; on held-out United States postings it never saw, it recognises 32%. In this sample only 41 postings (0.2%) are for hands-on trade jobs such as electrician, welder or fitter, and 19 (0.1%) for nursing or ward care, so most vocational courses cannot be scored from it.

        Limits

        Postings say what employers advertise, mostly in the formal sector, not everything a job needs. A skill outside the taxonomy is invisible, so the page lists what it could not read. Thresholds (15% core, 30 postings, 200-hour reference) are judgement calls, stated openly. Nothing yet shows that higher scores lead to more placements.
