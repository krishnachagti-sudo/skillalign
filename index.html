        Home / Your data

        Use your own job postings

        Have a spreadsheet of job ads from a job portal or your placement cell? Upload it and every score on the site is worked out from your ads. Nothing leaves your browser.

        What the file needs

        • A CSV file (in Excel: File → Save As → CSV).
        • Columns title (job title) and date (2025-08-14 or 14/08/2025).
        • A description column, or a skills column like "Python|SQL|Excel".
        • Optional: sector, district or city, state, employer (repeat ads from one employer are then counted once).
        • Two years of ads give trends; less still gives skill shares and course scores.

        Home / How it works

        How it works

        Every number is worked out by a small Python program running in your browser. There is no AI model and no server: the same data always gives the same answer, and every rule below can be checked by hand.

        The course score in one line

        Score = the share of what these jobs ask for that the course teaches with enough hours.

        score = 100 × Σ (share of ads asking for skill × credit for skill) ÷ Σ share, over skills in 15% or more of the ads. Credit is 1 when the course gives the skill at least its fair hours, less when it gives fewer, 0 when it is not taught.

        Grades: 75 and above Strong, 50–74 Partial, below 50 Weak. "Borderline" means the score's range crosses a grade line.

        1. Reading skills out of text

        A list of 101 skills with 820 ways of writing them, including ITI trades, business skills and Marathi words. In running text the longest known phrase of up to four words is looked up at each word; short ambiguous words count only in their proper form ("Excel", not "excel at"), and a skill right after "no", "not" or "without" is skipped. A job board's skills list ("python|sql|excel") is read item by item. Skills never come from the job title. Words it cannot read are listed for you.

        2. Measuring demand, honestly

        A skill's share is the fraction of job ads that mention it, shown with a 95% range. A change compares the latest four quarters with the four before, within the same job roles (a Mantel-Haenszel test), so a change in which roles are hiring is never read as a skill trend. The bar for "beyond chance" is raised for testing many skills at once (Bonferroni). A forecast is drawn only with six or more quarters.

        3. Scoring a course

        Only ads for the course's own jobs count, and an entry-level course is compared with entry-level jobs only. With fewer than 30 matching ads the course is not scored. Skills in 15% or more of the ads are core. Full credit for a core skill needs half of what a 200-hour reference course would give it, in proportion to its demand, and never less than 5 hours. When one module teaches two skills, its hours are split between them. So shrinking a course lowers its score, padding it with unrelated hours changes nothing, and adding hours to a core skill can only help. The score's 90% range comes from resampling employers 400 times.

        4. What to change

        • Add: a core skill the course does not teach.
        • Expand: taught, but with fewer hours than full credit needs.
        • Prepare: not core yet, but rising beyond chance and forecast to reach 15% within a year.
        • Reduce: taught, yet in under 5% of the ads and confidently under 15%.
        • Review: still asked for, but falling beyond chance within the same roles.

        Changes are listed most important first: by how many ads ask for the skill, raised (at most double) when it is rising beyond chance. Nothing in a mandatory module, and no basic skill (communication, English, teamwork, problem solving, computer use), is ever advised to cut: job ads under-report basics.

        Checked against the truth

        On practice data the true trends are known, so the method can be scored (full results). On trend-free data it reports almost no trends, even when the mix of job roles shifts or employers repost ads. It finds every large change and few modest ones; what it reports points the right way 98% of the time. The 90% score ranges contained the true score 85 to 100% of the time, and forecasts were within their 95% range 91% of the time in a backtest.

        Real data

        "Real job ads" are 19,194 Indian postings from January to June 2026, published by Fliar Research under CC BY-NC 4.0 (source), cleaned as described in the data notes. It is a 6% sample of companies, collected unevenly, so its numbers describe that sample, not the whole job market. Two quarters is too short for trends. The skill list recognises 50% of its skill items, and 32% on held-out United States postings it never saw. Only 41 ads (0.2%) are for hands-on trade jobs such as electrician, welder or fitter, so most vocational courses cannot be scored from it.

        Limits

        Job ads say what employers advertise, mostly in the formal sector, not everything a job needs. A skill outside the list is invisible, so the page shows what it could not read. Thresholds (15% core, 30 ads, 200-hour reference) are judgement calls, stated openly. Nothing yet shows that higher scores lead to more placements.
